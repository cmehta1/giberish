What a fragrance is made of

Every perfume is a composition of ingredients: petals and resins, woods and spices, and molecules first made in a laboratory. Here is where each one comes from, how it is made, how it smells, and where it lives in a fragrance.

How a fragrance is built

Top, heart and base

A fragrance is not one smell but three movements. Small, light molecules rise first; the heart arrives as they fade; heavy molecules stay for hours.

Timings are typical. Skin, climate and the amount you wear change them.

The fragrance wheel

Fifteen families

Perfumers group smells into families that sit side by side like colours on a wheel. Neighbours blend easily; opposites create tension and surprise.

Natural and synthetic

From the field and from the lab

Natural

Taken from flowers, leaves, roots, woods, seeds, peels and resins by physical processes such as distillation, pressing or extraction. Naturals are rich and complex: one rose oil holds hundreds of molecules.

Their harvest depends on soil, weather and hands, so every season smells a little different.

&

Synthetic

Molecules made by chemistry. Some copy a molecule found in nature (nature-identical); others smell like nothing that grows, such as clean musks and airy woods.

Synthetics spare endangered plants and animals, keep a fragrance consistent, and opened up whole new families of scent.

Almost every modern perfume uses both. “Natural” is not automatically safer, and “synthetic” is not automatically cheaper: some lab molecules cost more than gold.
